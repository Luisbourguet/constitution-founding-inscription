## Abstract

This article asks what kind of social object a national constitution is and how, as such an object, it institutionalizes a political community's answers to the invariants of the human condition. The thesis is that a constitution is a **founding inscription**: a sovereign, recursive, generative and maximally unamendable social object. The framework integrates Ferraris's New Realism (documentality), Bunge's and Romero's systemic materialism, Glenn's theory of legal traditions as transmitted normative information, Todorov's axes of alterity, and Anderson's and De Botton's functional reading of religion. The thesis is confronted, in an exploratory way, with constitutional orders from the civil-law, common-law, chthonic, Talmudic, Islamic, Hindu, East Asian, socialist and African traditions, drawing on the Comparative Constitutions Project. Three findings result: the founding inscription may be *enacted*, *revealed* or *distributed*, and these modes overlap; its hardness may be *textual*, *doctrinal* or *ritual*, and must be analysed along three distinct dimensions — procedural rigidity, substantive intangibility and effective stability; and everywhere the enacted constitution coexists with older or higher inscriptions, the decisive variable being the mode of their relation — denial, subordination or reconciliation.

## Method note / Nota metodológica

The selection of traditions follows H. P. Glenn's map; within each tradition, cases were chosen (i) for the public availability of their founding texts, (ii) to include at least one instance of coexistence between an enacted constitution and an older inscription of the tradition, and (iii) where possible, for their prominence in the comparative literature. The selection is illustrative and deliberately adversarial, not representative. Textual similarity across constitutions systematically overstates convergence of practice; every provision in these tables should be read together with the institutional practice that gives it force.
